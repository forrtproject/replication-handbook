Challenges for Repetitive Research in Computational Literary Studies

PR #78 · tracked changes

Tip

Key takeaways

Computational Literary Studies (CLS) analyses large collections of literary texts with statistical and machine-learning methods. Repetitive research is still rare in the field, partly because there is little prestige attached to it and few venues that require data and code sharing. Three specific challenges shape what is possible: earlier studies that rely on analog sources and qualitative methods cannot be reproduced exactly, copyright limits the sharing of corpora, and probabilistic methods such as topic models and generative language models do not give identical results across runs. Practical ways forward include treating the analog/digital divide as a continuum, sharing derived text formats instead of full texts, and documenting raw outputs of multiple runs.

After this chapter, you will know:

  • Which characteristics of Computational Literary Studies make reproductions and replications difficult?

  • How can copyright-protected texts be made available for repetitive research?

  • How can repetitive research deal with results from probabilistic methods?

Introduction

This chapter aims to introduce readers to the field of Computational Literary Studies (CLS) and to the specific challenges this field faces in the context of repetitive research. The chapter begins by providing a brief sketch of CLS as a field. This is followed by a discussion of several challenges that repetitive research currently faces in the context of CLS: working across the analog/digital divide when repeating older but still relevant studies; working with texts protected by copyright and making them available to others; and the widespread use of probabilistic methods and language models.1

Context: What is Computational Literary Studies?

Computational Literary Studies (CLS) is a field that, under various names, has a long history within the Digital Humanities (DH). Broadly speaking, CLS is concerned with developing, evaluating and using computational methods based on machine learning and statistics for the investigation of research questions in the field of literary history and literary studies.2 Among the most well-established methods in the field are stylometric authorship attribution, probabilistic topic modeling, sentiment analysis, literary network analysis, spatial analysis and mapping, as well as text re-use (Rebora, 2026). More generally, the field often relies on the automatic extraction of a specific set of textual features deemed relevant to a specific research question and their mobilization for clustering, classification or regression tasks. Recently, the field has adopted both encoder and decoder-based (Large) Language Models as a valuable way to engage deeply with the semantic and stylistic layers of literary texts (see e.g. Hicke & Mimno, 2023; Manjavacas Arevalo & Fonteyn, 2021; Pichler et al., 2025; Trilcke et al., 2025). The primary domain of application in CLS are literary texts such as novels, plays or poetry, both from the contemporary period (usually covered by copyright) and from earlier periods of literary activity (often with challenges related to OCR quality and historical states of language). CLS is particularly focused on detecting large-scale patterns and trends within the literary system and therefore typically relies on datasets containing literary texts (and relevant descriptive metadata) that vary in size but typically range in the hundreds to thousands of texts. The field is wide-ranging in geographical scope, so that rather than being limited to English texts, text collections often include texts in less-resourced languages or are multilingual in scope (see e.g. Schöch et al 2021).

Challenges for repetitive research in the context of CLS

Some of the challenges faced by repetitive research as an emerging research practice in other fields are also valid in the context of CLS. First of all, there is no widespread understanding of the usefulness of, or any measure of prestige associated with, repetitive research, certainly not to a degree that would be sufficient to trigger repetitive research as an established practice. Second, only a small number of venues for publication relevant to CLS enforce best practices of sharing data and code, thereby enabling repetitive research (Illmer, 2025), and include studies performing repetitive research in the description of the journal’s scope (a case in point is the Journal of Computational Literary Studies and, to a lesser degree, the Journal of Cultural Analytics). Finally, so far there exist really a very limited number of actual instances of repetitive research in the field, representing a limited range of experiences with the challenges involved or suitable solutions to address these challenges.

The analog/digital and qualitative/quantitative divides

In Literary Studies, as in many of the subfields of the Humanities, research questions remain relevant over long periods of time, and influential hypotheses that may be interesting to test may have been originally developed based on analog, printed sources. Such earlier studies also sometimes did not consider it relevant to specify which editions of the texts under investigation they used, or may have used multiple different editions available to them. In addition, whether older or recent, many publications in literary studies develop their hypotheses and results using qualitative, non-algorithmic methods.

In this context, reproductions (using identical data and methods to investigate the same research problem) are of course extremely difficult to implement. As a consequence, research in such scenarios is more likely to be a robustness reproduction of the research question (investigating the same research question with the same data but different methods) or may even better be described as follow-up research or conceptual replications (similar research question investigated using clearly different data and methods).

One example of such a study is an attempt to repeat a study of the French seventeenth-century dramatic author Jean Racine by Leo Spitzer, first developed and published almost 100 years earlier (Spitzer, 1931). The repeating study was able to model just under half of the qualitative stylistic phenomena described by Leo Spitzer using replicable queries, but concluded that Racine’s use of these phenomena was not, statistically speaking, different from their use by Racine’s contemporaries (Schöch, 2024).

The copyright problem

As mentioned above, CLS is interested both in the long-term historical trends observable in literary history and in more recent developments in the literary field. While building large and reliable collections of historical texts has its own challenges – for example concerning availability of good-quality texts in digital formats, or taking into account the way language has changed over time with respect to orthography, the meaning of words or grammatical structure –, at least texts published before 1920 can today generally be assumed to be in the public domain, that is not protected by copyright.3 Whenever CLS research is concerned with texts published after 1920, however, copyright immediately becomes an impediment both to the reuse of corpora others may have already built (but cannot share) and to sharing the corpora oneself has created for a particular study (so that others may re-use them). Building such corpora for research purposes is covered by the “Text and Data Mining Exception” in European copyright law (and by the fair use doctrine in some other jurisdictions), but making these corpora openly available to others, following best practices of Open Science, is not. Concerns around protecting personal data may apply as well.

Many studies published in the Journal of Computational Literary Studies face this issue and sometimes discuss this in their data availability section. For example, in a large-scale study of contemporary fanfiction, Neugarten (2025) writes: “To protect the privacy and copyright of authors in the fanfiction community, full-text works of fanfiction are not made available for reuse.” Röttermann et al. (2024) use an innovative solution to circumvent this issue at least to some extent. Rather than publishing the full texts of their corpus of 600 contemporary French novels, they publish detailed statistical data derived from the full texts. This data supports various kinds of statistical analyses of the lexical material, but does not allow reading and understanding the actual text. This strategy, inspired by endeavours such as the Hathi Trust Research Center’s Extracted Features Dataset (Jett et al., 2020), has been proposed by Schöch et al. (2020; for a recent survey, see Schöch (2026)). Such Derived Text Formats – which can operate via randomised word order or descriptive frequency statistics – allow strict replication to some extent, depending on the research methods used. But the very first step of creating the DTF cannot be made entirely transparent and some methods, especially those requiring the investigation of larger stylistic patterns in full sentences, cannot be supported by such approaches.

The corpus used by Langer et al. (2021) shows these issues of data availability very clearly. Before ca. 1830, their corpus based on the Gutenberg Project texts includes a very limited number of texts, due to limited availability of digital versions of texts. And after ca. 1930, the numbers decline again, due to copyright restrictions (see their Figure 2). In his replication and partial refutation of their study, Piper (2022) then reconstructs that dataset, but also uses two additional datasets that have better coverage after 1930, but are either limited to a specific form of derived dataset (the HathiTrust 1M dataset) or are not openly available for inspection and re-use (the Stanford and Chicago Corpus; see his Data section). Accordingly, while still a highly useful part of the documentation of his research, Piper’s published dataset can only include derived data and metadata rather than full texts.4

Using probabilistic methods

The third challenge for repetitive research in CLS concerns the widespread use of methods and resources that are probabilistic in nature, whether they are static or contextual word embeddings (word2vec or BERT), probabilistic methods such as topic modeling and neural network-based machine learning techniques, or generative Large Language Models. In each of these scenarios, and to different degrees, these methods do not allow strict reproduction even by the researchers working on a study themselves.

One relatively simple example includes topic modeling, a method that remains popular in CLS and where each run will give slightly different results, with small differences in parameters or preprocessing having possibly wide-ranging effects. Against best practices, quite often only one run is documented (see e.g. Völkl et al., 2022). In such a case, a replication effort aiming to establish whether the same results can be achieved, would need to run the same topic modeling analysis on the same data multiple times. Then, for example, the range of variation in the degree of similarity between top topic word lists from multiple runs, can be compared to the topic word lists reported in the original study, for example using Rank-Biased Overlap, in order to estimate whether the original study’s results appear to belong to the same distribution.

Even greater instability of results can be observed when using prompt-based interactions with generative LLMs for research, as is increasingly done. For example, Pichler et al. (2025) found that in the context of a sequence labeling task for CLS, “results are sensitive to data splits and prompt formulation”. While the same process may or may not be obtained in a replication study, given that – in this particular case – the result is a labeled sequence of tokens, it is relatively easy to check whether performance in a replication is identical to performance in the original study as well as checking for the degree of overlap in the kinds of errors that can be observed. Unfortunately, I am not aware of any study in CLS specifically aiming to replicate or reproduce findings from an earlier study using Topic Modeling or generative LLMs.

Conclusion: Ways forward

For each of the three specific challenges described, there are ways in which the CLS community of researchers may choose to push towards solutions. First of all, bridging the analog/digital and the qualitative/quantitative divide between earlier studies and repeating research will be helped by a more general acknowledgement that these are not binary distinctions, and that mixed-methods research is actually a rather widespread practice in CLS. Second, copyright may or may not change substantially in the medium term, but demonstrating the usefulness of Derived Text Formats for research in CLS could help establish this practice more widely in CLS and related fields, such as Corpus and Computational Linguistics and Natural Language Processing or Digital History. Third, establishing more rigorous standards of reporting results from probabilistic processes or using resources that are probabilistic in nature, in particular documenting raw outputs from multiple runs, will help mitigate the potentially problematic effects of such methods on repetitive research (and improve the quality of research more generally, in the process).

With regard to the more general challenges discussed above, one may be tempted to see them as a typical ‘chicken-and-egg’ problem: Without suitable venues to present and publish repetitive research, and without recognition of repetitive research as a valuable enterprise, the number of concrete examples and the range of experience is bound to stay limited. But without such examples and experiences, the need for dedicated publication venues doesn’t exist, and the usefulness of repetitive research cannot be demonstrated to fellow researchers.

Two strategies appear to be promising avenues to break out of this vicious circle in CLS: First, connecting the field to other fields where repetitive research is more established, and opening up avenues for repetitive research to happen, may provide an important motivation for engagement with the topic in CLS. Second, more explicitly making the point that repetitive research is really a best practice in the context of Open Science may also help. Open Science is well-established in DH and CLS, for example with respect to the FAIR data principles or regarding Open Access publishing, and showing that repetitive research is an integral part of Open Science will therefore be another important way to support repetitive research in CLS.
